[The last post](/blog/half-the-frames-in-my-recording-never-reached-the-video/) ended with a derived take holding 11,986 of the 23,896 frames a screencast had logged, a check that the 30 fps video picks the same file at every output frame either way, and one line admitting that no render was timed. The tempting follow-up is that the render got about twice as fast. I can't write it: nothing in the production record timed a render of that tutorial before or after the cut, and the 49.8% that was dropped is a share of files, not a share of anything the render spends time on. The reasoning behind Amdahl's law says why that matters — improving one part of a job pays off only in proportion to the time that part takes, and the untouched rest sets a ceiling however fast the improved part gets. His 1967 paper argued a narrower, empirical version of it about parallel hardware. Applying it to the steps of a video pipeline, and mapping which steps the cut can reach, is my extension.

## The paper behind the law has no equation in it

The copy I read is the 2007 reprint in the IEEE Solid-State Circuits Society's newsletter, two pages, of a conference paper from the AFIPS Conference Proceedings, vol. 30, 1967, pp. 483–485. The editors' note says it plainly: the paper "has no equations and only a single figure" (p. 19), and Amdahl redrew that figure for the reprint because the available hard copy was illegible.

What he argued is narrower and more empirical than the law that carries his name. The subject was hardware: whether interconnecting many processors beats building one faster one. The first piece of evidence was a single fraction — data-management housekeeping had stayed close to 40% of the executed instructions in production runs for about ten years, it looked sequential, and a dedicated environment might halve it but probably not cut it by three. "Overhead alone would then place an upper limit on throughput of five to seven times the sequential processing rate, even if the housekeeping were done in a separate processor" (p. 19). From that one number he concluded that effort on high parallel rates "is wasted unless it is accompanied by achievements in sequential processing rates of very nearly the same magnitude" (p. 19).

The rest of the paper piles on costs a clean model leaves out. Irregular boundaries, inhomogeneous interiors and state-dependent computation degrade a parallel machine "in the vicinity of one-half to one order of magnitude" against a regularized version of the problem, and two processors sharing memory need about 2.2 times the hardware to deliver about 1.8 times the performance (p. 20). The reprint also carries a 2007 caption by Justin Rattner that reads the figure as at least 25% sequential computation plus 10% coordination overhead, while Amdahl's own text puts the probable region at 25% data-management overhead and 10% of operations forced to be sequential — the caption swaps his two categories (p. 20).

## The modern formula needs a share of time, not a share of files

The textbook form came later, and applying it to one stage of a pipeline rather than to a processor is my move, not his. Let **p** be the fraction of the baseline wall-clock time spent in the part you speed up, and **s** how many times faster that part becomes. The rest, 1 − p, is untouched, so the new time is (1 − p) + p/s of the old, and the whole job runs **S** times faster:

S = 1 / ((1 − p) + p / s)

As s grows without limit, S approaches 1 / (1 − p). That ceiling fits Amdahl's own range: halve his 40% housekeeping to a fifth of the work and the bound is 5; cut it nearly threefold, to about a seventh, and it's about 7. He doesn't show that arithmetic — it's my reading of his numbers. The formula also rests on idealizations: a fixed workload, every other part unchanged, parts that run strictly one after another, and no new overhead from the change itself. The fixed workload is named in Rattner's caption, not by Amdahl; the missing overhead is what Amdahl's second page is about.

```text
hypothetical job: 100 time units, one stage takes 20 of them (p = 0.2)
stage twice as fast (s = 2):   80 + 20/2 = 90 units   → S = 100/90 ≈ 1.11
stage instant (s → ∞):         80 + 0    = 80 units   → S = 100/80 = 1.25, the ceiling
```

Every input in that formula is time. The 49.8% is 11,910 never-picked files over 23,896 logged ones, and it isn't p for any step: no step's time is known to be proportional to it.

## The steps meet the frame list in three different ways

The production scripts fix the order. A take is recorded live: Chrome screencasts the demo application while a script drives it and waits out each narration clip's length, so recording takes at least as long as the take, 511.5 seconds for the final one. `thin-take.mjs` writes the derived take. Then `final.sh` runs the rest one step at a time — data, layout and type checks, bundle, render, loudness, subtitles and chapters, cover, trainee guide, QA — and its header says "One render at a time". The checkpoint leaves one step to a person: the owner watching the result at normal speed.

The footer names three clocks: render only is step 5, the automated pipeline steps 2 to 8, the whole production steps 1 to 9.](../../assets/blog/half-the-frames-is-a-file-count-not-a-share-of-render-time/pipeline-diagram.svg "Step order from the production scripts. The boxes are equal because no step was timed; the stripes are a reading of the code, the file counts come from the take logs, and the footer names the three clocks a share of time can be measured against.")

Steps 2 to 4 visit or copy every entry of the list they're given, so their file work tracks its length. Thinning adds step 2 outright: `thin-take.mjs` walks every entry of the original log once and hard-links the files it keeps. `build-data.mjs` copies each frame of the take it's given into the studio's `public/` folder, as an APFS clone where it can, so it handles 11,986 files instead of 23,896. `remotion bundle` then copies the whole `public/` folder into the bundle file by file — the bundler's own code does a `copyDir` of it unless told to symlink, and `final.sh` doesn't. That folder also holds fonts, narration clips and material from other tutorial parts, so the take's own subfolder is 11,910 files lighter while the bundle copy as a whole shrinks by a share nobody counted.

Step 5 meets the list differently, and it's where the intuition breaks. Remotion opens browser tabs; each tab draws one output frame and screenshots it, and the frames are encoded to H.264. That work is per output frame, and the composition has 15,722 of them whichever list it reads. Each output frame shows one screencast JPEG, and the same 11,986 files reach the screen either way, so the images the render loads don't change. What the cut does change inside the render is the list itself: the props that carry it are smaller, and each frame's search through it takes one fewer halving. That's a real change in the render's work. How much time it moves is a question for a timed render, and there isn't one.

Steps 6, 8 and 9 work on the finished video. `finish.mjs` measures and re-encodes only the raw render's audio, to −16 LUFS, and copies the video stream untouched. QA re-reads the finished file and its companions: format, length, loudness, sound in every step's voice window, subtitles, chapters, cover, and a still before every click laid out on contact sheets. Step 7 sits apart: subtitles and chapters come from the narration's word timings, the guide from the same data, and the cover is one still rendered from the bundle. One playback at normal speed takes as long as the video, 8 minutes 44 seconds. A person reviewing it can take longer — pausing, going back, playing a line twice — and the record says only that the owner's review was still pending, not how long it took.

## A share of time depends on which clock you read

p is a share of a total, and this production has three totals worth keeping apart. The render alone is step 5. The automated pipeline is steps 2 to 8: `thin-take.mjs`, then everything `final.sh` runs, in order. The whole production adds the live capture before it and a person's review after it — and even that leaves out the narration and the demo setup that come before the take. A p measured against one of these clocks predicts a speed-up only for that clock. Steps 3 and 4 aren't inside the render at all, so against the render clock their share is zero; what reaches inside step 5 is the shorter list — smaller props, one fewer halving per frame. Against the pipeline clock, steps 3 and 4 have a share nobody measured. Against the production clock that share is smaller again, because capture and review add time the cut never touches.

## Overlapping stages break the additive arithmetic

The formula adds times, and `final.sh` really is additive between its steps: `set -euo pipefail`, one command after another. Inside the render step it isn't. Remotion's **concurrency** is the number of browser tabs rendering at once, and its documentation warns that past some point more tabs bring diminishing returns and an overloaded machine that can crash the render. That's the same shape as Amdahl's two shared-memory processors delivering 1.8 for 2.2 times the hardware, though his figure was about hardware, not tabs. Remotion also has **parallel encoding**: FFmpeg encodes finished frames while Chromium renders the following ones.

When drawing and encoding overlap, the render's length is set roughly by the slower of the two streams, plus the start and the finish — not by their sum. A faster encoder can then shorten the render by less than the encoder's own time, or by nothing, if drawing was the longer stream all along. So a faster encode doesn't guarantee a faster render, and a faster render doesn't speed up the production by the same factor: capture and review don't move with it.

Whether they overlapped here isn't in the record. The studio pins Remotion 4.0.532, and that version's renderer turns parallel encoding on for an H.264 render unless a free-memory estimate says the machine can't afford it; the documentation adds that the automatic switch-off was dropped in 4.0.533 and that parallel encoding became the plain default in 4.0.534. `final.sh` passes no flag either way and renders without `--log=verbose`, so the line that would say "Parallel encoding is enabled" or "disabled" was never written.

## The only render timing in the record isn't a baseline

The production notes contain exactly one render time: a pipeline test of another part of the same tutorial series that rendered 45 seconds of video in 28 seconds, with an estimate of about four minutes for the full part. It used a draft take, a temporary voice and a different composition, and it can't say what share of this tutorial's automated pipeline went into steps 3 and 4.

So p is unknown, and I'd rather leave it unknown than borrow it. The comparison that would measure it is small to describe: the same machine, the same composition and narration, the original take and the derived one, a clock around each step that `final.sh` already announces with a `==` header — it prints the headers and no times — and the render's verbose log to say whether encoding overlapped. That yields p against the render and against the automated pipeline. A share of the whole production would also need the capture and the review timed, and a review's length depends on the person doing it. I haven't run any of it. Until someone does, the prediction from the code is about work, not time: thinning adds a step of its own, shrinks the file work in steps 3 and 4 and the list the render searches in step 5, and doesn't touch the capture or the review.

## Fewer files is still worth keeping without a speed claim

The cut has value that doesn't depend on p. The private media archive for the final stores the derived take's 11,986 frames rather than 23,896, and every later copy of the take — into `public/`, into the bundle — carries half the files. Local disk didn't shrink from the cut itself, because the derived take is hard links to the same files, and no byte count compares the archive with an un-thinned one; the only size on record is the first, unused take's, 24,078 frames and 4.5 GB. Fewer files mattered on this Mac anyway: `build-data.mjs` records that an earlier route, the 2880×1800 screen video through Remotion's video component, failed on the full disk, and the archive wasn't downloaded again after upload because only 7–9 GB were free. A frame list that holds only frames the video can show is also easier to reason about than one where half the entries can never matter.

Those are claims about storage and clarity. They hold without a stopwatch, which is exactly why they shouldn't be dressed up as a speed-up.

A cut's speed-up is worth what its share of the clock allows, and until someone reads that clock, half the frames is only half the files.
